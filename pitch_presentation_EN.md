## Introduction (≈ 20 s) · *Slide 1*

My presentation has three parts: first, a dashboard of the French economy; then three current debates from the press; and finally, how this news could affect the indicators.

## Part 1 — The dashboard (≈ 2 min 20)

### Slide 1 — The magic square
To judge the situation, I compare each indicator to its **benchmark**, the way economists do, rather than to a year picked at random.

- **Growth:** 0.4% expected in 2026, while France's potential growth is estimated at **1.2%**. The economy is running below its capacity.
- **Unemployment:** 8.3%, above the structural rate, which the Banque de France, France's central bank, estimates at **7.8%**. This suggests a cyclical component, although the structural rate is itself an estimate with some uncertainty.
- **Inflation:** 3.1%, above the European Central Bank's **2%** target.
- **External balance:** the only good news. The current account is close to balance, with a deficit of about 10 billion euros over the past year.

### Slide 2 — The indicators and their benchmarks
The most worrying area is **public finances**. The deficit stands at 5.4% of GDP, against an EU rule of 3%. Debt is close to 120% of GDP, twice the 60% rule. Even before interest payments, the state spends more than it earns. This is called the **primary deficit**, and at 2.8 points of GDP it means the debt keeps rising. Interest costs would climb from **79 to 91 billion euros** next year. And markets now charge France **1.37 percentage points more than Germany** to lend to it over ten years. The rating agency Moody's will review France's credit rating on 23 October.

### Slide 3 — France compared with its neighbours
Inflation affects **the whole euro area**, and France is slightly below the average. On public finances, however, France stands out clearly from its neighbours: its deficit is the highest of the five countries, and its debt is second only to Italy's. The data are **consistent with the hypothesis** of a France-specific problem, but with five countries and a single year, we can't draw a firm conclusion. This is the backdrop to the three debates of the week.

## Part 2 — Press review: three topics

### 2a. Poverty (≈ 3 min 30)

**Slides 4 and 5 — The facts.** According to Insee, the French national statistics office, **15.4%** of the population lives below the poverty line, which is **9.8 million** people. That is the highest level since records began in 1996, and the rate stayed at this record in 2024.

**Slide 6 — Who is poor?** You are considered poor if you live on less than 60% of the median standard of living, so poverty here is **relative**. It affects more than a third of unemployed people and of single-parent families, and almost one self-employed worker in five.

**Slide 7 — On the left: "political choices".** According to *L'Essentiel de l'Éco*, poverty has risen from 13.8% to 15.4% since 2017, which means 1.2 million more poor people. The article blames the end of emergency inflation support and housing benefits that have not kept pace with prices. Inequality is also growing, with the Gini index rising from 0.281 to 0.297. And the official figure leaves out homeless people, students and the overseas territories.

**Slides 8 and 9 — The free-market view: "too much state".** For the IREF, a free-market think tank, relative poverty mostly measures inequality. Its preferred measure is extreme poverty, which fell from **36% to 10%** of the world's population between 1990 and 2015. France already collects **45.6%** of its GDP in taxes and social contributions, against 40% on average in the EU. In the IREF's view, more redistribution would discourage people from working.

**Slide 10 — In the middle: the OFCE.** Pierre Madec, an economist at the OFCE (a research centre at Sciences Po), separates four causes: a rising poverty line, demographics, how effective benefits are, and earnings from work. He also suggests looking at "disposable income after essentials", meaning what is left once unavoidable bills are paid.

**Transition:** cutting poverty without widening the deficit requires more revenue. That leads to the second debate: should the very rich pay more tax?

### 2b. The Zucman tax (≈ 3 min 30)

**Slide 14 — What is it?** It is a **minimum tax of 2%** on wealth above **100 million euros**, which would apply to about 1,800 households. According to the economist Gabriel Zucman, the very rich avoid tax through tax planning, by keeping their income inside holding companies. On 16 October, the National Assembly rejected it during the debate on the 2027 budget.

**Slide 15 — For.** Zucman says France has "a revenue problem" and estimates the tax would raise **20 billion euros** a year. According to Oxfam's calculations, the richest pay about 25% of their income in tax, against more than 50% for most French people. Seven Nobel laureates in economics support the tax.

**Slides 16 and 17 — Against.** For the Fondapol, another free-market think tank, the diagnosis is wrong: after redistribution, the gap between the richest and the poorest falls from 26 to 3.5. The revenue would also be very fragile. If just the three largest taxpayers left France, the yield would be cut in half. Seven economists, including Philippe Aghion, expect only 5 billion euros, and the Senate only 2 to 3 billion. Finally, a 2% rate with no cap could be ruled unconstitutional.

**Slide 18 — In the middle: the OFCE.** Yes, the very rich pay proportionally less tax. But taxing wealth that is hard to value is the wrong tool. The real risk is that entrepreneurs leave: when a major shareholder moves abroad, the company's payroll falls by about 31% within five years. The alternative is to tax capital gains when they are actually realised.

**Key point:** all three sides agree that tax planning through holding companies is a real problem; they disagree on the solution.

### 2c. Pensions and funded schemes (≈ 3 min 30)

**Slides 22 and 23 — The context.** Today, France pays 98% of its pensions on a pay-as-you-go basis: people in work pay for current retirees. But the number of workers per retiree has fallen from more than 4 in 1990 to 2.5 today. In the 2027 presidential campaign, Édouard Philippe wants 15% of pensions to be funded, Gabriel Attal proposes 1,000 euros for every newborn, and even the Socialist MP Philippe Brun suggests a public pension fund.

**Slide 24 — For: the Fondapol.** It proposes a compulsory funded tier that would pay a quarter of private-sector pensions. Pay-as-you-go returns about 1% a year above inflation. The Fondapol assumes 4% for a funded scheme, and the Swedish public pension fund achieved 5.8%. In the long run, contributions could fall by more than 6 points. To deal with market crashes, a collective funded scheme can smooth returns between generations.

**Slide 25 — Against: the CGT trade union.** A funded scheme would break solidarity, because periods of unemployment or maternity leave would no longer earn pension rights. It would also expose pensions to stock-market risk, as in the Netherlands in 2008. Above all, people would have to **pay twice**: once for current pensions and once more for their own savings. The economist Eric Weil puts the cost of the transition at 300 to 400 billion euros.

**Slide 26 — Who would pay?** Every financing option has a drawback. The reserves of Agirc-Arrco, the private-sector supplementary pension scheme, about 91 billion euros, are meant for crises. Selling the state's shareholdings in companies would cost it their dividends. And raising pensions by less than inflation makes today's retirees pay. So there would be a "sacrificed generation": ours.

**Slide 27 — In the middle: Thesmar and Landier.** These two economists support a funded top-up, but reject the idea of a "miracle cure". The pension deficit accounts for about 3 points of GDP out of a total deficit of about 5. Only the retirement age or the level of pensions can reduce it.

## Part 3 — Conclusion: how this news could affect the indicators (≈ 1 min 20)

To finish, let's go back to the dashboard.

- **Deficit and debt:** rejecting the Zucman tax and the measures on retirees deprives the budget of revenue. Estimates range from 2 to 20 billion euros for the Zucman tax, plus 1.4 billion for the retirees' tax allowance. The 5% deficit target for 2027 becomes harder to hit.
- **Interest rates:** if the budget is not credible, the gap with Germany could widen further, especially if Moody's downgrades France on 23 October. Interest costs, already expected at 91 billion euros, would rise even more.
- **Purchasing power, consumption and poverty:** smaller pension increases and frozen benefits weigh on low-income households. Confidence, already low at 86, and consumption could suffer, and poverty could stay at its record level.
- **In the long run:** a funded pension tier would increase savings invested in shares, but it would not reduce the deficit for decades.

In short, this week's three debates all come back to one question: **how to finance a generous welfare model while debt keeps growing.**

### Notes for questions
- **Why benchmarks rather than a 10-year average?** The 10-year average is distorted by Covid and the price surge. Economists compare to the target, the EU rules and the economy's potential.
- **Potential growth and structural unemployment** are estimates: 1.2% according to the HCFP, France's independent fiscal council (opinion 2026-5), and 7.8% according to the Banque de France (for 2025).
- **Inflation: 3.1% or 3.4%?** 3.1% is Insee's French consumer price index (CPI). 3.4% is the harmonised European index (HICP), used to compare countries.
- **Unemployment a year ago (7.6%)** reflects Insee's new coverage, which has included Mayotte since August 2026.
- **Why not draw a firmer conclusion from the European comparison?** There are few observations (5 countries), a single year, no control group and no statistical test. Definitions may also differ between countries. The data point in the direction of the hypothesis without proving it.
- **The IFRAP**, another think tank, also calculates that the interest rate paid on the debt is now higher than growth (r − g ≈ +0.8 points in 2026). This is a think-tank figure, not an official one.
